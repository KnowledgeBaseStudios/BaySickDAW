### 1. What it means: "Overshoot magnitude proportional to raw jump"
In a simple physics model, the harder you "throw" the needle, the further it will bounce.
* **The Logic:** If the signal jumps from silence ($-20$ VU) to $0$ VU instantly, the needle gains a massive amount of momentum. 
* **The Problem:** Without "braking" logic, the needle will fly way past $0$ VU—perhaps hitting $+5$ or $+10$—and oscillate wildly before settling. 
* **"Erratic" Look:** In the digital world, if the bounce is too large, it looks like a glitch or a "broken" spring rather than a professional piece of gear.

### 2. Does our previous model fix this?
**Partially.** The second-order dynamics model includes a **damping ratio ($z$)**, which is your primary tool to fight erratic behavior. 
* If $z$ is too low ($< 0.5$), the overshoot is huge and erratic.
* If $z$ is too high ($> 1.0$), the needle never overshoots at all, making it look sluggish and "digital."

However, to truly fix the "proportional overshoot" issue, you need to add **non-linear damping** or **clamping**.

---

### 3. The Professional Fix: "The Soft Limit"
To make it look natural even during huge jumps, you should implement these three refinements to the physics model:

#### A. Input Velocity Limiting
Real analog needles have physical air resistance and mechanical friction that increases as they move faster. You can "cap" the maximum velocity the needle is allowed to reach. This prevents a "silence-to-loud" jump from turning the needle into a blur.

#### B. The "End-Pin" Simulation
Hardware VU meters have a physical brass pin at the end of the scale (usually around $+3$ or $+5$ VU). 
* **The Fix:** Instead of letting the needle swing to $+10$, you create a "hard stop" at $+3.5$. 
* **The Natural Touch:** When the needle hits this limit, set the velocity to zero (or a tiny negative value for a "rebound") so it looks like it physically smacked the pin.

#### C. Variable Damping (The "Heavy" Needle)
You can make the damping ratio ($z$) slightly higher when the needle is moving upward and slightly lower when it is falling. This mimics the behavior of a needle that "fights" to move up but "drops" naturally.
