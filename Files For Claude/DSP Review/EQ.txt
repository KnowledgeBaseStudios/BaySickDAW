Since you’re building **Vibesynth** to mirror the professional workflow of a high-end DAW, the EQ can't just be a standard digital filter. To get that "stock FL" or "Pro-Q" feel, you need **Topology-Preserving Transform (TPT)** filters for zero-delay feedback and a specific **Coefficient Smoothing** architecture to prevent clicks during automation.

Here is the professional breakdown for the **EqualizerDSP**.

---

### 1. The Core Engine (State-Variable TPT)
Standard digital filters (IIR) often sound "cramped" near the Nyquist frequency and can become unstable or "pop" when you sweep the cutoff frequency quickly.
* **The Logic:** Use the **Zavalishin TPT** model (available in JUCE as `juce::dsp::StateVariableTPTFilter`). 
* **Why:** This model mimics analog circuits by preserving the phase relationships between components. It stays perfectly stable even if you automate a 20Hz to 20kHz sweep in a single bar.

### 2. Band Architecture (The 7-Band Standard)
A professional EQ should have at least 7 bands with specialized roles:
* **Band 1 & 7:** Switchable between **High/Low Pass** and **High/Low Shelf**. (24dB/octave slopes).
* **Bands 2-6:** **Peaking/Bell** filters with adjustable **Q (Bandwidth)**.
* **Pro Tip:** For the "Peaking" bands, use a **Proportional Q** algorithm. This means the Q gets narrower as you increase the Gain, which feels much more musical and "hardware-like."

### 3. Magnitude Calculation (For the UI)
Since you are building a custom DAW, your UI will eventually need to draw the EQ curve.
* **The Logic:** Your DSP class should include a `getMagnitudeForFrequency(float frequency)` method.
* **The Math:** You must multiply the magnitudes of every active band together at that specific frequency coordinate to return the final visual curve.
    $$Magnitude_{Total} = \prod_{i=1}^{n} |H_i(f)|$$

### 4. Oversampling & Anti-Cramping
Digital EQs often lose their shape as they approach 20kHz (this is called "cramping").
* **The Fix:** Use **Oversampling (2x)** or implement **Orfanidis Optimization**. This ensures that a 15kHz boost looks and sounds like a symmetrical bell curve rather than a skewed digital mess.
